THE NIGHT THE LIBRARY STAYED OPEN

A short story written for testing the Readora e-reader.
It is an original sample, free to use and share.



CHAPTER I.

The Key on the Blue Ribbon

Mama Funke had kept the church library for nineteen years, and in all that time she had never once forgotten to lock the door. So when the rain came down on Thursday evening, heavy enough to turn the road outside St. Mark's into a brown river, and she found herself standing under the mango tree with her umbrella in one hand and no key in the other, she stood very still and tried to remember where her mind had gone.

The key lived on a blue ribbon. The ribbon lived around her neck. The ribbon was there now, damp against her collar, but the key that should have hung from it was missing, and the little knot she tied every morning had come loose like a tired shoelace.

"Lord," she said to the mango tree, "you know I am too old for this."

Behind her, the library windows were still glowing. She had switched off the big lights, but the small lamp over the returns desk was on, the way she always left it, so that anyone passing would know that books lived there and were waiting. Through the glass she could see the long shelves, the reading table with its scratched surface, and the wooden box where children dropped the books they had finished.

She could go home. Her house was only ten minutes away when the road was dry, and the door would still be standing open tomorrow morning. Nobody stole books in this street. People stole generators and phone chargers and, once, an entire goat, but nobody had ever stolen a book.

Still, she did not like it. A library left open overnight felt like a child left alone in the market. She folded her umbrella, shook the water from it, and went back inside to wait out the storm and look for the key.

The room smelled the way it always smelled in the rainy season: paper, dust, a little damp, and the sweet ghost of the mango leaves the wind had blown under the door. She switched the big lights on again. They buzzed and flickered, as they had done since the bishop's visit three years earlier, when somebody had promised to fix them.

The key was not on the desk. It was not in the returns box, though she put her whole arm in and felt along the bottom among the bookmarks and sweet wrappers. It was not on the floor beside the shelves of Christian living, or under the table, or in the pocket of the cardigan she kept on the back of her chair for cold mornings.

She sat down at last, out of breath, and that was when she heard the knock.



CHAPTER II.

The Boy at the Door

It was a careful knock, three small taps, the knock of someone who had been raised to respect a closed door even when it was standing open. Mama Funke looked up and saw a boy in the doorway, soaked through, holding a plastic bag against his chest as though it contained something alive.

"Good evening, ma," he said. "Please, are you still open?"

She knew him a little. He was one of the Adeyemi children, the third or fourth, the quiet one who came on Saturday mornings and read in the corner by the fan until his older sister came to drag him home. Tobi. His name was Tobi, and he was eleven, and he always returned his books on time, which made him one of her favourite people in the world.

"We are closed," she said, and then, because he looked so disappointed and so wet, "but the door is open, and I am here, so come in before you melt."

He came in and stood dripping on the mat. He opened the plastic bag very carefully and took out a book wrapped in a second bag, and inside that, in a third. It was a thick book of stories about the sea, with a blue cover that had faded almost to white.

"It is due tomorrow," he said. "But tomorrow we are travelling to my grandmother in Ibadan, and I did not want it to be late."

Mama Funke took the book and turned it over in her hands. It was perfectly dry. She looked at the boy, at the water running from his hair into his eyes, and at the three bags folded neatly in his hand.

"You walked here in this rain," she said, "to return a book one day early."

"It is not early if I am not here tomorrow," Tobi said, very reasonably. "Then it would be on time."

She stamped the return in the ledger and wrote the date in her slow, round handwriting, the way she had written it for nineteen years. Then she looked at the clock, and at the window, where the rain showed no sign of stopping, and she made a decision that she would think about for a long time afterwards.

"Your mother knows you are here?"

"My sister knows," he said, which was not quite the same thing.

"Then you will stay until the rain stops, and I will walk you home. Sit. There is a towel in the cupboard behind the hymn books."

He found the towel and sat in his corner by the fan, though the fan was not on, and looked around the library as if he had never seen it at night before. Which, she supposed, he had not. Nobody had. In nineteen years she had never kept the library open after six o'clock.

"Ma," he said after a while, "why are you still here?"

"I have lost my key," she said. "And until I find it, I cannot lock the door, and until I lock the door, I cannot go home."

He nodded slowly, as though this were a sensible problem with a sensible answer, and then he stood up and folded the towel.

"Then I will help you look," he said.



CHAPTER III.

What the Shelves Remembered

They searched together. Tobi was small enough to look under the low shelves where Mama Funke's knees would no longer take her, and he was thorough in the way only children and accountants are thorough. He found two pencils, a hair clip, a coin from a country neither of them recognised, and a folded church bulletin from four Easters ago. He did not find the key.

"When did you last have it?" he asked.

"This morning," she said. "I opened the door with it. I always open the door with it."

"And then?"

She thought. She had opened the door. She had switched on the lights, which had flickered. She had put her bag on the desk. Mrs. Okafor had come at nine with a box of books from her late husband's study, and they had spent the whole morning unpacking it together, sorting the good books from the ones that had been eaten by termites, telling each other stories about Mr. Okafor, who had been a difficult man and a generous one.

"The box," she said. "The Okafor box."

The box was on the bottom shelf in the back room, where she put donations until she had time to give them numbers and cards. It was half empty now. The books that were left were the ones she had not yet decided about: old maths textbooks, a cookery book in Portuguese, three copies of the same biography of a footballer, and a heavy dictionary with a cracked spine.

Tobi pulled the books out one by one and stacked them on the floor. At the bottom of the box, under a loose sheet of brown paper, there was no key. But there was an envelope, unsealed, with a single line written on the front in blue ink: For the library, when it is needed.

Mama Funke sat down on the little stool by the shelf and held the envelope for a long moment before she opened it. Inside were twelve photographs and a letter.

The photographs were of the library. Not as it was now, but as it had been: the same room, the same windows, but with different shelves, a different table, and children she did not recognise sitting in rows on the floor. In one picture a young man with a serious face stood beside a blackboard on which someone had written, in careful capitals, OUR LIBRARY IS OPEN TO ALL.

"Who is that?" Tobi asked, pointing.

She turned the photograph over. On the back, in the same blue ink: Emmanuel Okafor, first librarian, 1971.

"That," she said, "is the man whose books we unpacked this morning."



CHAPTER IV.

The Letter

The letter was short. Mr. Okafor had written it in the last year of his life, and his handwriting shook a little, but every word was clear.

To whoever keeps the library after me, it began. I started this room with forty books and a borrowed key. The key was never mine. It belonged to the church, and the books belonged to anyone who wanted them. I have kept these photographs so that you will know that the room has always been more than a room. When you are tired, and you will be tired, look at the children on the floor. They are grown now. Some of them are doctors. One of them is a bishop, though he still owes me a book. Keep the door open as long as you can. A library is only a library when someone is in it.

Mama Funke read it twice and then gave it to Tobi, who read it once very slowly, moving his lips a little at the hard words.

"He was the first librarian," Tobi said. "Before you."

"Before me, and before the woman before me," she said. "I did not know. Nobody told me. I thought the library started when the women's guild bought the shelves."

Outside, the rain had softened to a whisper. The road would still be a river, but a smaller one. Tobi looked at the photograph of the children on the floor, and then around the room, at the empty corner by the fan where he always sat on Saturdays.

"Ma," he said, "if he wanted the door to stay open, maybe it is not so bad that you lost the key."

She laughed, for the first time that evening, a real laugh that surprised them both.

"That is very good theology," she said, "and very bad security."

But she did not get up to keep looking. She sat on the little stool with the letter in her lap and thought about forty books and a borrowed key, and about nineteen years of locking a door that someone else had built to be open.



CHAPTER V.

Morning

The key, in the end, was in her umbrella. It had slipped from the ribbon when she opened the umbrella under the mango tree, and caught in the folds of the cloth, and it fell out onto the floor with a small bright sound when Tobi picked the umbrella up to carry it for her on the way home.

They both looked at it for a while. Then Mama Funke picked it up, and turned off the big lights, and left the small lamp burning over the returns desk, and locked the door.

She walked Tobi home through the shallow water, holding the umbrella over both of them, and explained to his mother, who had been worried and then angry and then, when she heard the whole story, mostly amused, that her son had walked through a storm to return a book one day early and should be given extra rice for it.

In the morning Mama Funke came back to the library at the usual time. She opened the door with the key on its blue ribbon, tied with a new knot, a double one. She switched on the lights, which flickered. And then, before she did anything else, she took the photograph of Emmanuel Okafor and his blackboard and pinned it to the wall above the returns desk, where everyone who came in would see it.

OUR LIBRARY IS OPEN TO ALL.

On Saturday, the Adeyemis were still in Ibadan, and the corner by the fan was empty. But on the Saturday after that, Tobi came in at nine o'clock exactly, with his sister behind him, and saw the photograph, and smiled, and went to his corner, and read.

That evening Mama Funke stayed until seven. Nobody asked her to. A woman from the choir came in at half past six to borrow a cookery book, and a young man who had just finished university came in to read the newspapers because the light at home was off, and two little girls came in with their grandmother and sat on the floor, in a row, exactly like the children in the photograph.

She locked the door at seven with the key on the blue ribbon, and she did not mind at all.

A library is only a library when someone is in it.



THE END
